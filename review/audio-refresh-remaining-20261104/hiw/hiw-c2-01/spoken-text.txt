Administrative standards are often defended as a means of insulating decisions from personal preference. Their legitimacy, however, remains contingent on the purposes they serve and the circumstances in which they are applied. A rule may constrain discretion without eliminating the judgement required to interpret an unusual case. Treating every classification as provisional does not entail abandoning consistency; it acknowledges that categories can obscure distinctions relevant to a defensible decision. The crucial question is whether departures from routine are authorised through reasons that others can examine. Where decisions are reversible, institutions can learn from objections without presenting every revision as an admission of incompetence. Accountability therefore depends on making interpretations contestable while preserving a coherent explanation of why comparable cases receive comparable treatment.
