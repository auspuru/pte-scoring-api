Conserving a historic building involves interpreting continuity rather than simply arresting change. Materials decay, functions evolve and successive alterations may acquire historical significance of their own. Restoring a structure to an imagined original condition can erase evidence of the very processes that make it valuable. Authenticity is therefore not exhausted by the survival of individual components; it also concerns the intelligibility of relationships between fabric, use and setting. This broader account does not license unrestricted redevelopment. It calls for a discriminating judgement about which interventions sustain the building's significance and which merely substitute a convenient narrative for its complexity. Documentation should make those judgements traceable, allowing later conservators to distinguish inherited features from deliberate additions and to reassess decisions whose assumptions may no longer be persuasive.
