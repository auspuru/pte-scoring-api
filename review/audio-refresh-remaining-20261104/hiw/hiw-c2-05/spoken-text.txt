A translation may be lexically accurate yet alter the relationship between a speaker and an audience. Forms of address, understated objections and strategically unresolved references carry pragmatic implications that cannot always be transferred through equivalent vocabulary. The translator must decide which ambiguities are incidental and which are constitutive of the original argument. Resolving every uncertainty can impose a clarity that the source deliberately withholds; reproducing every unfamiliar construction can make a coherent voice seem needlessly obscure. Fidelity consequently requires a negotiated balance between intelligibility and the preservation of meaningful difference. Such judgement is neither arbitrary nor reducible to a universal formula. It becomes defensible when the translator can explain how a particular choice sustains the force of an utterance, including what it insinuates without explicitly asserting.
